## Summary
### Background
There are an estimated 1·3–4·0 million cases of cholera and 20 000–140 000 cholera-related deaths worldwide each year. The rice-based cholera toxin B subunit (CTB) vaccine, MucoRice-CTB, is an oral candidate vaccine that does not require a cold chain, has shown efficacy in animal models, and could be of benefit in places where there is a paucity of medical infrastructure. We aim to assess the safety, tolerability, and immunogenicity of MucoRice-CTB in humans.
### Methods
We did a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled, dose-escalation, phase 1 study at one centre in Tokyo, Japan. Eligible participants were healthy adult men with measurable serum and faecal antibodies against CTB at screening. Participants were excluded if they had allergy to rice; history of cholera or travellers' diarrhoea; poorly controlled constipation; abnormal results on hepatic, renal, or haematological screening tests; use of any over-the-counter drugs within 7 days before first administration; inability to use a medically acceptable means of contraception; or other reasons by medical judgment of the investigator. Three dose cohorts of participants were randomly assigned by block to receive oral MucoRice-CTB (1 g, 3 g, or 6 g) or placebo (1 g, 3 g, or 6 g), once every 2 weeks for 8 weeks (for a total of 4 doses). The dose groups were performed sequentially, and each dose cohort was completed before the higher dose cohort began. All medical staff, participants, and most trial staff were masked to treatment allocation. The primary outcomes were safety and tolerability, measured by 12-lead electrocardiogram; vital signs; haematology, biochemistry, and urinalysis; rice protein-specific serum IgE antibody concentration; and monitoring of adverse events. Participants were assessed at baseline and at 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 16 weeks after the first administration of vaccine or placebo. The safety analysis set included all participants enrolled in the trial who received at least one dose of the study drug or placebo and were compliant with good clinical practice. The full analysis population included all participants enrolled in the trial who received at least one dose of the study drug and for whom any data were obtained after the start of study drug administration. Meta-genomic analysis of study participants was performed using bacterial DNA from faecal samples before vaccination. This trial is registered with UMIN.ac.jp, UMIN000018001.
### Findings
Between June 23, 2015, and May 31, 2016, 226 participants were recruited and assessed for eligibility. 166 participants were excluded based on health condition or schedule. We then randomly selected 60 male volunteers aged 20–40 years who were enrolled and assigned to MucoRice-CTB (10 participants assigned to 1 g, 10 participants assigned to 3 g, and 10 participants assigned to 6 g), or placebo (10 participants assigned to 1 g, 10 participants assigned to 3 g, and 10 participants assigned to 6 g). All participants received at least one dose of study drug or placebo and were included in the safety analyses. Two participants given MucoRice-CTB 3 g and one participant given MucoRice-CTB 6 g were lost to follow-up and excluded from the efficacy analysis. Serum CTB-specific IgG and IgA antibody concentrations in participants who received 6 g MucoRice-CTB increased significantly in both a time-dependent and dose-dependent manner compared with those in the placebo groups (p for interaction=0·002 for IgG, p=0·004 for IgA). Genome analysis of subjects' faeces before vaccination revealed that compared to non-responders, responders had a gut microbiota of higher diversity with the presence of *Escherichia coli* and *Shigella* spp. 28 (93%) of 30 participants who received MucoRice-CTB at any dose had at least one adverse event during the study period, compared with 30 (100%) of 30 participants given placebo. Grade 3 or higher adverse events were reported in four participants in the MucoRice-CTB group (5 events) and four participants in the placebo group (10 events). The most common serious adverse event was haemoglobin decreased (2 events in 2 participants in the pooled MucoRice-CTB group, 2 events in 2 participants in the placebo group; all grade 3).
### Interpretation
Participants given MucoRice-CTB showed increased CTB-specific serum IgG and IgA antibody concentrations without inducing serious adverse events, indicating that MucoRice-CTB could be a safe and potent vaccine to prevent diarrhoeal disease. MucoRice-CTB induced neutralising antibodies against diarrhoeal toxins in a gut microbiota-dependent manner. A similar phase 1 trial will be done with participants of other ethnicities to substantiate our findings.

Research in context
**Evidence before this study**
Diarrhoeal disease remains a global health concern. Roughly 1·7 billion cases of diarrhoea occur worldwide each year, resulting in about 525 000 deaths. Most of those affected are children younger than 5 years residing in low-income countries. About half of the cases are caused by pathogenic bacteria including *Vibrio cholerae* and enterotoxigenic *Escherichia coli* (ETEC) that produce enterotoxins. Two oral cholera vaccines containing inactivated *V cholerae* (Shanchol and Euvichol) with a recombinant cholera toxin B subunit (CTB; Dukoral) have been licensed for the prevention of cholera and travellers' diarrhoea. Because *E coli* heat-labile enterotoxin (LT) and shiga toxin produced by ETEC that cause severe diarrhoea in children in low-income countries and in travellers to those countries possess high homology with cholera toxin (CT), it has been shown that oral vaccination with whole killed bacteria-based Dukoral exhibited cross-protection against ETEC. Despite the availability of a whole killed bacteria-based vaccine, the creation of a cold chain-free oral vaccine was both anticipated and needed, especially for low-income countries. We thus used our rice plant-based vaccine development platform to develop a strain of rice that expresses CTB in its protein bodies (MucoRice-CTB) for the prolonged stable storage of vaccine without requiring refrigeration. Using a murine model, it was shown that a cold chain-free MucoRice-CTB effectively prevented intestinal fluid accumulation caused by CT produced by *V cholerae* and LT from ETEC. In addition, the oral immunogenicity and efficacy of MucoRice-CTB have been demonstrated in experimental mouse, pig, and non-human primate models.
**Added value of this study**
Our findings show that oral vaccination with MucoRice-CTB induced antigen-specific serum IgG and IgA antibody responses in healthy volunteers without inducing severe adverse events. The antibodies induced by MucoRice-CTB possess the ability to neutralise CT and LT intoxication. This is the first study to evaluate a cereal plant-based oral cholera vaccine (MucoRice-CTB) in humans. This study also provides evidence that the composition of the gut microbiota is associated with the immune response to orally administered vaccines, including MucoRice-CTB. We demonstrate the feasibility of using a cereal plant-based, cold chain-free and needle-free vaccine in humans.
**Implications of all the available evidence**
Our study suggests that MucoRice-CTB might be a potentially useful vaccine against both cholera and diarrhoea induced by LT. Furthermore, some of the healthy Japanese participants who exhibited a high baseline titre of pre-existing, CTB-specific serum IgG antibodies probably possessed *E coli* and *Shigella* (or their related) species in their commensal flora, despite our records showing that none of the 58 participants ever travelled outside Japan. These points need to be formally tested by future clinical investigation. Because this is a first-in-human study with a limited number of healthy Japanese volunteers, the gut microbiota analysis must be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, our results suggest that the diversity of the gut microbiota and the presence of specific bacterial species, some of which are pathogenic bacteria, influence the antigen-specific antibody responses induced by oral MucoRice-CTB. Further examination of the influence of the gut microbiota on the immune responses to MucoRice-CTB is warranted in a larger number of participants. Because the present study population consisted of healthy Japanese males aged 20–40 years, due to the absence of potential toxicity data for female reproductive tissue induced by oral MucoRice-CTB, the effectiveness of MucoRice-CTB should be evaluated in both sexes, and in higher-income and low-income countries that include endemic regions. It has been shown that the composition of the gut microbiota differs between individuals from higher-income and low-income countries. Indeed, two oral vaccines against cholera (eg, Dukoral, a killed cholera vaccine, and CDV 103-HgR, a live cholera vaccine) have decreased effectiveness in low-income settings. To this end, if MucoRice-CTB is to be tested in regions where cholera is endemic, differences in the gut microbiota must be taken into consideration.

Repeated oral dose GLP toxicity studies in rats (600 mg/body per day) and dogs (3 g/body per day) showed no MucoRice-CTB-related adverse effects (data not published).
Building on these previous studies, we designed a first-in-human study to assess the safety, tolerability, and immunogenicity of MucoRice-CTB, and the effect the gut microbiota has on the immune response to MucoRice-CTB.
## Methods
### Study design
We did a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled, dose-escalation, phase 1 study (IMSUT-MR1501) to evaluate the safety, tolerability, and immunogenicity of MucoRice-CTB in healthy Japanese adult men. We did the study at IMSUT hospital (Tokyo, Japan).
Because faecal microbiota analyses were a part of clinical study outside of the phase 1 study, we obtained informed consent from all participants, and have provided the details of assays in the appendix (pp 24–81). The clinical research protocols were approved and received ethical clearance from the institutional review board of IMSUT, The University of Tokyo, Japan (permit 27-41-0917 and 27-48-0128; protocol available in the appendix pp 24–81).
### Participants
Eligible participants were healthy Japanese men aged 20–40 years with measurable serum and faecal antibodies against CTB at screening. Women were excluded as the safety of MucoRice-CTB has not been established in women, especially with respect to reproductive tissue. Exclusion criteria included allergy to rice; history of cholera or travellers' diarrhoea; poorly controlled constipation; abnormal results on hepatic, renal, or haematological screening tests; use of any prescription drugs within 14 days before the first administration, or any over-the-counter drugs within 7 days before the first administration; inability to use a medically acceptable means of contraception; or other reasons by medical judgment of the investigator. All participants were recruited publically through websites or targeted email, and provided written informed consent.
### Randomisation and masking
Participants were randomly assigned by block randomisation to receive oral MucoRice-CTB (1 g, 3 g, or 6 g) or placebo (1 g, 3 g, or 6 g), once every 2 weeks for 8 weeks (for a total of 4 doses). The dose groups were performed sequentially, and each dose cohort was completed before the higher dose cohort began. To assess potential acute risks carefully, the first administration in each cohort was done over 2 days with 20 participants divided between these days (ten in the MucoRice-CTB group and ten in the placebo group; six participants on day 1, 14 participants on day 2). We therefore set two block sizes (6 and 14) with a 1:1 allocation ratio in each block. No stratification was used. All medical staff, participants, and most trial staff were masked to treatment. The Efficacy and Safety Evaluation Committee reviewed the data collected during the period from the start of the study to week 8 under blind conditions, evaluated the safety endpoints including severity and number of adverse events, and then discussed whether to proceed to a cohort with a larger dose.

### Outcomes
The primary outcome was safety and tolerability of MucoRice-CTB in healthy adult Japanese men, measured by 12-lead ECG; vital signs; haematology, biochemistry, and urinalysis; rice protein-specific serum IgE antibody; and monitoring of adverse events. We applied a modified version of a high-standard grading scale for adverse events in healthy adult volunteers enrolled in preventive vaccine clinical trials (USA Food and Drug Administration [FDA] Guidance for Industry: Toxicity Grading Scale for Healthy Adult and Adolescent Volunteers Enrolled in Preventive Vaccine Clinical Trials). Adverse events were tabulated using lowest level terms and preferred terms of the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities terminology (Japanese translation). Secondary outcomes were efficacy indices of MucoRice-CTB, which included CTB protein-specific serum IgG or IgA antibodies, CTB protein-specific faecal secretory IgA (SIgA) antibody, and B lymphocytes. ### Statistical analysis
The sample size of each cohort provides sufficient precision to detect adverse events with a probability of 25%; based on binomial distribution, such a frequent adverse event will occur in at least one participant with a probability of at least 90%.
The safety analysis set included all participants enrolled in the trial who received at least one dose of the study drug or placebo. Participants who were not compliant with good clinical practice were excluded. The full analysis population included all participants enrolled in the trial who received at least one dose of the study drug, and excluded participants for whom no data were obtained after the start of study drug administration. The per protocol population included all participants in the full analysis population who had no major protocol deviations. We report results from the full analysis population for the efficacy evaluation because no specific results were obtained in the per protocol population. Detailed methods for MucoRice-CTB immunogenicity and faecal microbiota analysis are described in the appendix (pp 13–23). Statistical analysis was done with SAS, version 9.4. This trial is registered with UMIN.ac.jp, UMIN000018001.

Seven participants reported ten adverse events of grade 3 or higher in the pooled placebo group, and four participants reported five adverse events of grade 3 or higher in the MucoRice-CTB group (table 2). The most frequently reported grade 3 or higher adverse event was haemoglobin decreased (2 events in 2 participants in the pooled placebo group, 2 events in 2 participants in the MucoRice-CTB group; all grade 3 as change from baseline was 2·1–5·0 mg/dL). Incidence of grade 3 or higher adverse events did not appear to be vaccine-related. The grading scale used in this study was strict because it was designed for preventive vaccines and healthy volunteers; therefore the adverse events can be considered less severe than those of the same grade in other clinical trials.
Serum CTB-specific IgG and IgA antibody concentrations in participants who received 6 g MucoRice-CTB increased significantly in both a time-dependent and dose-dependent manner compared with those in the placebo groups (p for interaction=0·004, figure 2; appendix p 6). More participants in the MucoRice-CTB group than in the placebo group showed a change of 4 times or more from the baseline (reciprocal log2 titre) in serum IgG and serum IgA concentrations (appendix p 7). It should be noted that the calculated 95% CIs for visits 6 and 7 (6 and 8 weeks after first immunisation) in the 1 g placebo cohort were 2·8–60·0%, which is too wide to be interpreted. In this regard, the CTB-specific serum IgA antibody responses in the 1 g placebo group were probably accidental outcomes (p=0·218, figure 2). We found no differences in CTB-specific faecal IgA antibody concentrations between the MucoRice-CTB and placebo groups, due to technical challenges in obtaining and processing faecal samples (p for interaction=0·323, appendix p 8).

To test whether CTB-specific serum IgG and IgA antibodies induced by MucoRice-CTB provided cross-reactive immunity to LTB, we obtained serum from six randomly selected participants (5 from the MucoRice-CTB 6 g group; one from the 6 g placebo group). Serum IgG and IgA antibodies from the MucoRice-CTB group both had cross-reactivity with LTB, and this cross-reactivity correlated with the CTB-specific IgG and IgA antibody responses (appendix p 9).
Oral immunisation with MucoRice-CTB induced CTB-specific IgG and IgA antibodies and prevented the binding of CTB and LTB to GM1. When CTB or LTB was pre-incubated with serum from study participants and subjected to a GM1 receptor binding inhibition assay, the binding of CTB and LTB to GM1 receptor was inhibited by serum (1:40 dilution) from participants who received MucoRice-CTB 6 g, but not by the same dilution of serum from participants who received placebo (appendix p 9). Furthermore, binding inhibition was associated with antigen-specific antibody titre (appendix p 9).
CT or LT pretreated with serum from participants administered MucoRice-CTB caused no morphological changes in the CHO cells (appendix p 10). By contrast, CT or LT pretreated with serum from a participant in the placebo group caused marked elongation of the CHO cells, similar to that observed with CT or LT alone (appendix p 10).
Participants in whom maximum CTB-specific serum IgG and IgA antibody titres increased by at least 4 times from baseline were defined as responders (appendix p 4). Although dose escalation resulted in a positive seroconversion in all three MucoRice-CTB groups (figure 2, appendix p 7), 27 participants given MucoRice-CTB who were not lost to follow-up could be categorised as responders (16 participants) and non-responders (11 participants) on the basis of serum CTB-specific IgG and IgA antibody responses (figure 3C). Time-dependent changes between responders and non-responders were clearly observed for CTB-specific IgA and IgG antibody responses (figure 3A). Among responders, serum CTB-specific IgA and IgG antibodies increased after the first dose of MucoRice-CTB and reached maximum concentrations 8 weeks later for IgA and 16 weeks later for IgG (figure 3A). No antigen-specific IgA or IgG antibody response was observed in responders. Although pre-existing CTB-specific serum IgG, IgA, and faecal IgA antibodies were detected before vaccination, the participants who had a near or higher average of pre-existing CTB-specific serum IgG antibody titre tended to fall into the responder group (appendix p 4). To predict vaccine responsiveness as a percentage expression of the average of pre-existing CTB-specific serum IgG antibody titres, the optimal cutoff value was set at 81·5% (figure 3B). Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) was 0·643 in all 27 participants given MucoRice-CTB who were not lost to follow-up. However, AUC-ROC was much higher in 17 participants who received MucoRice-CTB at 3 g or 6 g, at 0·803. We observed a clear difference between responders and non-responders in changes of CTB-specific serum IgG and IgA antibody responses when stratified by the optimal cutoff value (figure 3C). However, the changes in the samples from participants who received MucoRice-CTB 1 g were statistically insufficient to define the responder and non-responder groups (figure 3C).

We found higher inter-sample diversity (β-diversity) in the responder group, indicating that similarity was higher among the non-responders.
